# What To Do When a Competitor Changes Their Pricing (A Decision Framework)

You find out a competitor just cut their prices by 30%.

Or they added a free tier. Or they dropped the free tier and went all-paid. Or they restructured their entire pricing model.

Now what?

Most teams do one of two things: panic and change their own pricing immediately, or do nothing and hope it doesn't matter. Both are usually wrong.

I've been through four of these situations now — once on the wrong end of a price cut I missed entirely, and three times where I caught it early enough to actually think it through. Here's the framework I use.

---

## First: Don't Assume It's a Threat

The instinct when a competitor lowers prices is to think "they're attacking us." Sometimes that's true. Often it's not.

Competitor pricing changes happen for a bunch of reasons that have nothing to do with you:

- They raised money and can afford to subsidize growth for a while
- They're trying to hit a specific metric (MAU, ARR logo count) before a fundraise
- They had churn problems in a price-sensitive segment and are trying to fix it
- They did user research and found their pricing was confusing or too high
- Their board pushed them to grow faster

A startup that cuts prices after a Series B isn't necessarily targeting your customers. They might be targeting a different segment entirely.

The first question to ask is: **who is this price change for?**

---

## Second: Look at What Actually Changed, Not Just the Number

"Competitor cut prices 30%" is a headline. It's not a strategy.

Read the details. What I look for:

**Which plan changed?** If they cut their entry plan, they're going after small customers. If they restructured the enterprise tier, that's a different conversation. If they killed a tier entirely, they're simplifying for a reason.

**What happened to limits?** Sometimes a price cut comes with tighter limits. $79 → $49/month sounds like a price cut until you notice the seat limit dropped from 5 to 2. The price went down but the value went down more.

**Did the free tier change?** Free tier changes are the most strategic move in SaaS pricing. Adding a free tier means they're trying to go bottom-up, get into more companies, and expand from within. Killing a free tier usually means their PLG motion failed and they're going sales-led.

**Is the page different now?** Not just the number. Does the page feel different — more enterprise, more startup, different pain points in the copy? The price change might be the symptom of a repositioning.

---

## Third: Wait 48 Hours Before Doing Anything

This is the one I had to learn the hard way.

The first time I saw a competitor drop their price significantly, I immediately started drafting a response email to customers and redoing our pricing page. Wasted a full day. Ended up not changing anything.

The 48-hour rule: give yourself time to actually process it before reacting. Talk to a few customers. Check if anyone even noticed. Look at whether your inbound changed at all.

Most of the time, a competitor pricing change that feels massive from the inside is invisible to your customers. They're not comparison shopping daily. They're using tools that are working for them.

---

## The Three-Response Framework

After you've waited and thought it through, you're deciding between three options:

### Option A: Do Nothing

This is the right call more often than you'd think.

Do nothing if:
- The segment they're targeting isn't your core ICP
- Your win rate hasn't changed
- Your customers aren't asking about it
- The price change came with limitations that make it a worse deal overall

Doing nothing doesn't mean being passive. It means continuing to execute your actual strategy instead of reacting to someone else's.

### Option B: Respond in Positioning, Not Price

This is what I'd recommend in most cases where you do need to respond.

If a competitor cuts prices and you have customers who care, the answer is usually to make your value clearer — not to match their price.

Why you're worth more. What you do that they don't. Which customers you're actually for. This is usually better than a price war.

Price wars are almost always destructive for everyone. Even if you win — you retain the customer — you've permanently lowered the perceived value of what you do. And whoever started the price cut can usually sustain it longer than you can.

### Option C: Match or Beat Them

The narrow case where this is right: you're truly head-to-head, same ICP, same features, and you're losing deals on price specifically.

Even then, I'd explore whether you can respond with a promotion or a segment-specific price rather than changing your public pricing.

Changing public pricing is high-commitment. It's hard to walk back without looking like you're raising prices. A time-limited promotion or a new plan tier is easier to adjust.

## The Monitoring Problem

Here's the thing: all of this assumes you found out about the pricing change at all.

I went three months once without realizing a competitor had restructured their entire pricing page. I found out during a sales call when a prospect said "well, [competitor] is doing $X now." I had no response. Not great.

The competitor didn't announce it. Didn't put out a press release. Just quietly changed the page on a Tuesday.

That's what Google Alerts misses — it only catches things mentioned on other sites. A pricing page change that nobody writes about doesn't show up in any alert.

The framework above only helps if you know there's a change to respond to.

## FAQ

**Should I always match a competitor's price cut?**

No. Price matching makes sense only if you're losing deals specifically because of price, and both products are genuinely comparable. Matching for its own sake trains customers to shop by price, which is a race you probably don't want to run.

**How do I know if a pricing change is hurting my conversion?**

Check your sign-up numbers from the period after the change compared to the same period before. If sign-ups held steady, the change probably didn't hit you. If they dropped, run a few customer calls to find out if pricing came up.

**What if they added features alongside the price cut?**

That's a more serious move. A price cut with added features means they're improving value and reducing price simultaneously — that's genuine competitive pressure. In that case, move up the timeline on your roadmap conversations and think about what differentiators you can accelerate.

**My competitor raised their prices. Is that an opportunity?**

Usually yes, but a smaller one than you'd think. Some of their customers will shop around. But most customers stay. The opportunity is mostly in capturing new evaluators who are now reconsidering. Make sure your messaging addresses the comparison directly.

**How often should I be checking competitor pricing pages?**

Weekly is right for most teams. Pricing changes tend to happen at the start of a quarter, after fundraises, or after major product releases. Weekly monitoring catches them within a week without overwhelming you with noise.
