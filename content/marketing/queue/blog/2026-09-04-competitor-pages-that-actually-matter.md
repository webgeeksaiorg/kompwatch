# The 4 Competitor Pages That Actually Matter (And Why You're Watching the Wrong Ones)

Most teams monitoring competitors are watching their blog.

It's an easy mistake. The blog is active. New posts show up. There's motion. Feels productive to track it.

It's almost completely useless for competitive intelligence.

I tracked 6 competitors for about a year before I figured out where the actual signal was. Here's what I learned.

---

## What I Was Watching vs. What Changed

When I first set up monitoring, I added every page I thought was important:

- Homepage
- Blog
- Changelog
- About page
- Pricing page

In 6 months, the changes that mattered — the ones I actually needed to know about — were on two pages. The rest was noise.

The homepage changed 3 times. All cosmetic. Hero image swaps, some copy tweaks. Zero competitive relevance.

The blog published 40+ posts. Reading every one would have taken days. Maybe 2 were genuinely useful competitive signals. You can't find those 2 without reading all 40.

The pricing page changed 11 times. Every single one was relevant.

---

## The 4 Pages Worth Monitoring

### 1. The Pricing Page

Obviously. But not just the headline number.

The real signal is in the details that disappear quietly:
- Free tier gets killed (or capped harder)
- Annual discount shrinks from 30% to 20%
- "Contact us for pricing" replaces the self-serve number
- A plan tier gets added or removed

These are strategic moves. They reflect how a competitor thinks about their market. "Contact us" replacing a price usually means they're moving upmarket or they got tired of small deals.

None of this shows up in Google Alerts because none of it generates a news story.

### 2. The Features/Product Page

Not because you're watching for feature launches — those usually go in changelogs and press releases.

You're watching for what they *stop mentioning*.

When a feature disappears from the marketing page, one of three things happened: it failed and they quietly killed it, it became a lower-tier item, or they repositioned around something else.

Any of those is useful. If they killed a feature you're about to build, that's a real signal. If they deprioritized something users cared about, there's a gap.

### 3. The Homepage Hero

Just the hero. Not the whole page.

The hero is where positioning lives. When a company changes their hero, they're telling you something real: either their messaging wasn't working, their ICP shifted, or they're responding to a competitive threat.

I've caught two competitors pivot to a new target segment just from watching hero copy changes. One swapped from "built for startups" to "enterprise-ready" over about 3 months. That was worth knowing.

### 4. The Jobs/Careers Page

This one gets ignored. It's the most forward-looking signal available.

If a competitor just posted three ML engineer roles and a "Head of AI" position, they're building something. You know it 6-9 months before they announce it.

Same with sales roles: 5 new BDR postings in a market you're both targeting means they're ramping up. New "Solutions Engineer" roles usually means they're going enterprise.

Job postings are the only place competitors accidentally tell you what they're doing next.

## What You Should Probably Stop Watching

**Their blog.** Unless they publish a product update or pricing announcement, it's SEO content. Not useful for CI.

**Their Twitter/social.** Marketing noise. Brand announcements you'll hear about anyway. Low signal.

**Their changelog.** Useful only if they publish one religiously and you can process the volume. Most can't.

**Their "About" and "Team" pages.** Only worth a quarterly glance to see if leadership changed.

---

## The Volume Problem

Here's the actual issue: if you're trying to manually check these 4 pages across 5-6 competitors, that's 20-24 URLs every week.

My Monday morning ritual was opening 12 tabs. It took about an hour. I'd make notes, update a spreadsheet, flag anything for the team.

That worked fine until I realized I was opening those tabs every 2 weeks, not every week. And then every 3 weeks. Life gets busy.

The fix was automating the check — not adding more tabs to the list.

For monitoring the right 4 pages per competitor, you don't need a $500/month enterprise CI platform. You need something that will just tell you when those pages change and what specifically changed.

## FAQ

**Why not just use Google Alerts?**

Google Alerts tracks mentions of your competitor's name on other websites — news, press, social. It doesn't watch their actual website. A pricing page change that they never announced anywhere will never show up in Google Alerts.

**How often should I check competitor pages?**

Weekly for pricing and features. Bi-weekly for homepage and jobs. Daily is overkill for most teams and creates alert fatigue.

**What if a competitor uses JavaScript/React and my monitoring tool can't scrape it?**

This is a real problem. Basic tools that just fetch HTML miss a lot of modern SaaS sites. You need headless-browser-based monitoring that renders the full page. Worth checking before you pick a tool.

**How many competitors should I be watching?**

3-5 direct competitors. More than that and the volume becomes hard to process even with automation. Focus on the 3-5 who you actually lose deals to, not the ones you've heard of.

**Do I need to watch every page they have?**

No. Just the 4 above. 4 pages × 5 competitors = 20 URLs. Manageable. Anything more and you're creating busywork.
