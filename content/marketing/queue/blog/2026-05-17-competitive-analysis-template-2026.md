# Competitive Analysis Template (2026): Free Copy-Paste Framework for SaaS Teams

Most competitive analysis docs die within 60 days. They start as a heroic 40-page Notion page, then a competitor changes pricing, the doc goes stale, sales stops trusting it, and everyone quietly reverts to "I think they charge around $X, maybe?" on calls.

This template fixes that. It's structured to be short enough to actually maintain, opinionated enough to be useful on a sales call, and paired with a lightweight process to keep it current after week 1.

Copy it into Notion, Google Docs, or Coda. Fill it in for 3 competitors (not 12 — we'll get to that). Then set up change alerts so it doesn't rot.

## How to Use This Template (Step by Step)

### Step 1: Pick 3 competitors — not 12

The single biggest failure mode: analyzing every competitor your PMM has ever heard of. Twelve superficial analyses lose to three deep ones every time.

**How to pick the right 3:** ask sales for the last 20 lost deals. Which competitor names come up? Usually you'll see 2–4 names 80% of the time. Those are your real competitors. The rest are noise.

If you don't have sales-call data, look at:
- G2 "Also considered" data for your category
- Search results for `[your category] alternatives`
- Reddit threads where users compare tools in your space

### Step 2: Fill in the fixed facts first

Pricing, positioning headline, ICP language, employee count. This part takes 30–45 minutes per competitor. Do it once, cite your sources, then almost never re-do it from scratch.

Two rules for pricing capture:
1. **Include the date you verified it.** Pricing goes stale in weeks.
2. **Screenshot the pricing page.** Vendors love to change pricing quietly and pretend it was always that way.

### Step 3: Rate strengths and weaknesses honestly

The temptation is to make your product look better on every dimension. Resist it. Sales reps will read this. If it doesn't match their real-world experience, they'll ignore the whole document.

If a competitor is genuinely stronger on a dimension, say so. Then figure out how you position around it (Step 6).

### Step 4: Map win/loss patterns from actual deals

Don't invent this. Pull it from your CRM. Look at the last 30 days of closed-won and closed-lost against each competitor. Two questions:
- **When we win against them, what's the customer profile + situation?**
- **When they win against us, same question.**

Write two sentences each. Not more. Reps need to remember this on a call.

### Step 5: Draft objection responses your reps will actually use

For each competitor, list the 3 objections your reps hear most. Write one crisp response per objection.

Bad response: "We're actually better because of our AI-powered platform's comprehensive features that enable seamless integration."

Good response: "Their $99 tier looks cheaper, but it caps at 5 competitors and 1 user. For your team of 6, you'd end up on their $299 tier — which is more expensive than us."

### Step 6: Compress each competitor into a one-line summary

The 20-word thing your rep says when a competitor comes up mid-call. If you can't compress it to 20 words, the positioning is muddy and needs more work.

**Example:** *"Klue is built for 100+ person enterprise GTM teams with dedicated CI analysts. Different product for a different customer."*

### Step 7: Set up change alerts (this is the step teams skip)

Here's the honest truth about competitive analysis: the doc is worth nothing if it's out of date. And most docs are out of date within 60 days.

The fix isn't discipline — you're not going to remember to re-check 3 competitors' pricing pages every Monday. The fix is automation. Point a monitoring tool at each competitor's:

- **Pricing page** — catches tier changes, price hikes, free plan removals
- **Features page** — catches new capabilities you'll get asked about
- **Homepage / positioning page** — catches messaging pivots that signal strategy shifts

When any of those change, you get pinged. You spend 5 minutes updating the relevant row. Log it in Section 9 so your team sees the doc is being maintained.

This is what turns a competitive analysis template from a one-time exercise into an actual living document.

## What Makes This Template Different from 2020-Era Templates

Most templates you'll find online were written pre-2022 and haven't been updated. They ask for:

- 4-page SWOT analyses (nobody reads them)
- Detailed feature matrices with 40 rows (impossible to keep current)
- "Company culture" and "brand perception" sections (unmeasurable, irrelevant on a sales call)

The 2026 version cuts all of that. What actually matters in a modern SaaS sales cycle:

1. **Pricing accuracy** — buyers Google pricing before calls. If your rep quotes stale competitor pricing, credibility gone.
2. **Positioning clarity** — buyers evaluate 3–4 tools. Your rep has 90 seconds to explain the difference.
3. **Freshness** — competitors ship weekly. A quarterly analysis is a stale analysis.

The template above optimizes for those three things and nothing else.

**FAQ**

**How many competitors should I analyze?**
Three. Maybe four. Pick from your last 20 lost deals — whichever names actually come up. Twelve analyses that no one maintains lose to three that stay current.

**How often should I update the analysis?**
The pricing and features sections should update within a week of any competitor change (which is why you need alerts on those pages). The positioning, ICP, and win/loss sections review quarterly.

**What's the difference between a competitive analysis and a battlecard?**
The analysis is the full document — sections 1 through 9 above. The battlecard is a condensed one-page version reps use on live calls (mostly sections 7 and 8). Same underlying data, different format for different consumption context. We have a [free battlecard template](/blog/competitive-battlecard-template) that pairs with this analysis.

**Should the analysis be shared with the whole company?**
Internal only. You're documenting your weaknesses and your rebuttals to competitor claims. Don't put it in a public Notion space or a shared Google Drive folder with external collaborators.

**How do I keep the pricing section accurate without checking manually every week?**
Set up automated monitoring on each competitor's pricing page. When it changes, you get an alert with a plain-English summary of what changed. [KompWatch](/pricing) does this — free plan monitors 2 competitors, Pro monitors 10. Takes 5 minutes to set up per competitor.

**What if a competitor doesn't publish pricing?**
Note "Contact sales" in the table and add any pricing signals you can find: G2 reviews sometimes mention contract sizes, LinkedIn job listings for AEs occasionally mention quota (which implies contract size), and prospects will often tell you what a competitor quoted them. Log the source and date.

**Do I need a competitive intelligence tool for this?**
For the analysis itself — no, a doc tool is fine. For keeping it current — yes, some form of change monitoring is what separates a living document from a corpse. It doesn't have to be an expensive CI platform (Crayon, Klue). A lightweight monitoring tool that pings you on pricing/features/homepage changes is enough for most teams under 50 people.

**How long should the completed analysis be per competitor?**
One page. Maybe two if you have complex objection-handling. If it's longer, your reps won't read it and the whole exercise fails. The template above is designed to fit on one page per competitor.
