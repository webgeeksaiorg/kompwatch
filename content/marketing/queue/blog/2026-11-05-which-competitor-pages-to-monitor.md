# Which Competitor Pages Actually Matter to Monitor (Most Teams Get This Wrong)

When I first set up competitor monitoring, I added everything.

Homepage. Pricing. About page. Blog. Press releases. Careers. Twitter. I had 11 pages per competitor across 6 competitors. 66 pages being watched.

The result was about 40 change notifications per week, 90% of which were CSS tweaks, cookie banner updates, and hero image rotations. I stopped reading the alerts within 3 weeks.

This is the most common mistake I see. Not "teams not monitoring competitors" — teams monitoring the wrong things.

Here's how I think about it now.

---

## The only question that matters: does this change tell you something?

A change on a competitor's website is signal if it tells you something about their strategy, their customers, or their direction.

A change is noise if it tells you they updated a stock photo.

That framing cuts the page list dramatically.

---

## Pages that consistently produce signal

### Pricing page

Obvious, but most teams only watch the top-level pricing page. The stuff that actually matters is often nested: plan comparison tables, feature gating changes, FAQ sections ("do you offer annual billing?"), and enterprise contact triggers.

Pricing changes are almost never random. A price increase usually means they found a segment that'll pay. A new tier usually means they're trying to serve a customer they currently lose. A free tier disappearing means unit economics got painful.

Each of those is worth knowing before it comes up on a sales call.

The practical thing: watch the full pricing page, not just the headline numbers.

### Features / product page

This one takes some setup because feature pages vary wildly. Some companies have a dedicated `/features` page. Others bury everything in `/product` or fold it into the homepage. Worth finding it.

Feature page changes are slower-moving than pricing, but meaningful. When a competitor adds a feature category to their nav, they're either shipping something new or repositioning existing functionality to compete in a new segment. Both are worth tracking.

The specific signal I watch for: new sections appearing (usually means new feature or new use case emphasis), old sections disappearing (usually means they're cutting scope or moving upmarket), and copy shifts ("for teams" becoming "for enterprises").

### Integrations page

Underrated. Almost nobody monitors this.

Integration launches are product strategy made visible. If a competitor just added a native Salesforce integration, they're going after enterprise sales teams. If they added Shopify + Klaviyo, they're going after e-commerce. If they added Slack and Notion, they want PLG.

This matters especially because integration launches are often quiet. There's no press release. The page just quietly gets a new tile. If you're watching it, you get 4-6 weeks of lead time before they start announcing it in sales conversations. If you're not, you find out when a prospect asks why you don't have it.

### Customer stories / case studies page

Slower-moving than pricing or features, but highly strategic.

New case studies tell you which verticals and company sizes they're closing deals in. If three new case studies in a row are all series B fintech companies, that's not random — they're winning there consistently and probably have a sales motion around it.

They're also useful for understanding which pain points they're leading with in messaging. A case study headline is positioning by definition.

### Homepage hero

Not the full homepage. Just the hero section: headline, sub-headline, and primary CTA.

Hero copy changes are strategic. They A/B test aggressively, but when they commit to a new headline, it reflects what's resonating with their target segment. The classic signal: "enterprise solution" becoming "simple tool for growing teams." That tells you they're repositioning, probably because enterprise closed rate was disappointing.

The full homepage is too noisy (too many elements that change). The hero is usually the most intentional 50 words on the site.

## Pages that sound important but aren't

### Blog

Competitor blogs are almost universally noise for monitoring purposes.

Every blog post triggers a change. Post frequency varies. Topic selection is often SEO-driven, not strategy-driven. By the time a strategic direction shows up in a blog post, you're probably already behind.

Exception: if a competitor does genuine product-focused content (release notes blog, developer blog), that's worth watching. But the standard "content marketing blog"? Skip it.

### Press releases / news

You'll see the actual announcement elsewhere before the monitoring catches it. Too slow, too low-signal for the volume of change events you get.

### Careers / jobs page

Tricky. Job listings are real signal — a sudden cluster of ML engineer postings tells you they're building something AI-related — but job pages are genuinely noisy. Listings get added, removed, re-added. The signal-to-noise is bad unless you're doing specific searches (keyword filtering for "AI" or "enterprise").

The workaround I use: instead of watching their careers page, run a manual check every few weeks. 10 minutes. Look for clustering in job titles. If you see 5 sales engineering posts when they had 1 before, that's a signal worth following.

### Social media

Twitter, LinkedIn — too much content volume, too low a signal ratio. Social posts are usually content marketing or announcements you'll see anyway. The monitoring noise is not worth it.

## The page list I actually use

For each competitor:

1. `/pricing` (full page)
2. `/features` or `/product` (wherever they put the feature overview)
3. `/integrations` (or `/integrations` section of the product page)
4. `/customers` or `/case-studies`
5. Homepage hero only (top 500px of the DOM, nothing else)

Five pages. Three to five competitors. That's 15-25 pages total. With good alerts, that's maybe 3-8 meaningful changes per week — small enough to actually read and act on.

---

## Why specificity matters more than coverage

There's a version of competitive intelligence where you're watching everything and feeding it into a giant database. That's the Crayon model, the Klue model. It works for teams with a dedicated CI analyst who's paid to synthesize a river of information.

For a 10-person team, or a solo PMM, that model breaks immediately. The river becomes noise, the notifications get ignored, the tool goes unused.

The goal isn't coverage. The goal is a small number of changes you actually read and understand.

Starting with 5 pages per competitor and expanding if you see clear signal gaps is a much better approach than starting with everything and trying to filter down. By the time you're filtering, the habit of ignoring alerts is already formed.

---

## Practical setup

If you're starting from scratch: pick your top 3 competitors, add their pricing and features pages, let it run for two weeks. See what changes. See if you read the alerts.

Expand from there based on what felt useful. Don't build the full 25-page setup until you've proven the smaller version actually changes how you work.

That's honestly the main thing. A system you use beats a comprehensive system you don't.

## FAQ

**What if my competitor doesn't have a dedicated pricing page?**

Watch the page they use to sell — homepage, demo request page, or /plans. The signal is usually still there, just less structured.

**How often should I check competitor pages?**

For pricing and features: twice a week minimum. Daily is fine if you're in an active competitive market. For case studies and hero: weekly is enough.

**What if my competitor is a large company with a complex site?**

Pick the pages that reflect their go-to-market motion toward your segment. You don't need to watch their enterprise product if you're competing in SMB. Focus on what's relevant to deals you're actually in.

**Should I monitor competitor G2/Capterra profiles?**

Review pages can be useful for understanding positioning shifts, but they change slowly. Quarterly manual checks are probably enough — the monitoring overhead isn't worth it for most teams.
